## Description

<!-- Please only use this template for submitting enhancement requests -->

**What would you like to be added**:

A way to pace how fast Kueue releases pods to the downstream scheduler, so it never puts more admitted-but-unscheduled pods ("in flight") in front of the scheduler than it can bind.

This builds on the `PodsScheduled` tracking from [KEP-13502](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/kueue/tree/main/keps/13502-unscheduled-pods-timeout) and is not an extension of `blockAdmission`. Proposed shape, open for discussion:

- **A global in-flight limit** in the Kueue `Configuration`, for example `maxInFlightPods`. In flight means pods of admitted Workloads that are ungated but not yet bound (`spec.nodeName == ""` / `PodScheduled != True`). Per-ClusterQueue and cohort limits are a possible follow-up.
- **Pod-level pacing for Workloads that do not need gang semantics.** After admission, Kueue keeps the pods behind a scheduling gate and removes it from pods in batches as the in-flight count drains, as TAS already does with per-pod gates. This is what makes a single Workload with thousands of pods safe to admit.
- **Workload-level cap for gang Workloads**, which need all their pods to start together and cannot be released in pieces. Kueue skips such a Workload for the cycle if `inFlightPods + workloadPodCount` would exceed the limit. It always admits when the in-flight count is 0, so a Workload larger than the limit cannot starve.
- Metrics for the current in-flight count, pods held behind the gate, Workloads held, and time held.

**Why is this needed**:

Kueue admits on quota (nominal plus borrowable), not on whether the scheduler can place the pods. When quota is large, one admission can ungate hundreds to thousands of pods at once. In front of a throughput-limited scheduler (for example, one that binds roughly 3 to 5 pods per second) this causes a flood. The pods arrive together, cannot be bound fast enough, sit admitted but unschedulable, and keep the scheduler busy. The goal is for the release rate to follow the bind rate.

Existing mechanisms do not cover this:

- **Quota** caps total concurrency, not the rate of release. Lowering it to N also lowers steady-state throughput to N.
- **`waitForPodsReady` with `blockAdmission`** waits and blocks admission for the whole scheduler, releases a Workload's entire pod set at once, and blocks every queue if one Workload never becomes ready.
- **`unscheduledTimeout` (KEP-13502)** evicts and requeues the whole Workload on timeout, which floods again when it is re-admitted. It also runs only behind an Alpha gate.
- **ProvisioningRequest AdmissionCheck** needs a Cluster Autoscaler that handles ProvisioningRequests. With a custom scheduler or no autoscaler, the check stays Pending and wedges the queue.
- **`AdmissionGatedBy` plus a small controller** that removes the gate while the unscheduled count is below N works today as a soft cap, and is the baseline this proposal has to beat. Every operator ends up owning such a controller, with cluster-wide Pod watch RBAC and accounting that must stay in sync with Kueue. The gate is also per Workload, so it leaves three gaps:
  1. **A single large Workload.** A Job with thousands of pods is released at once, and no Workload-level gate can pace it. Only pod-level pacing can.
  2. **Capacity that arrives slowly.** With an autoscaler and constrained capacity, the bind rate is the node arrival rate, and thousands of unschedulable pods keep the scheduler and autoscaler busy.
  3. **Isolation between tenants.** A ClusterQueue that releases many pods at once slows scheduling for other queues that are within their own quota. Quota and FairSharing act at admission and do not prevent this.

Open questions:

- How are pods held behind a gate for each integration? Plain pods already use a scheduling gate, but Jobs are held through `suspend`, so pacing their pods needs a gate added at pod creation.
- How does pacing interact with `waitForPodsReady`, preemption and eviction, and with the quota already charged to the admitted Workload?
- Does the limit require `unscheduledTimeout`, so pods that no node can fit do not stay in flight indefinitely?

### Comment by [@sohankunkerkar](https://github.com/sohankunkerkar) — 2026-10-11T19:47:21Z

I think this belongs in core: before admitting, Kueue counts the pods of admitted Workloads that aren't fully scheduled yet, and skips the Workload for that cycle if the count would go over the limit (always admitting when the count is 0, so a Workload larger than the limit isn't starved).
[KEP-13502](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/kueue/tree/main/keps/13502-unscheduled-pods-timeout) already tracks which admitted Workloads have unscheduled pods, so this can build on it, but today it runs only behind an Alpha gate with `unscheduledTimeout` set. I wouldn't extend [`blockAdmission`](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/kueue/tree/main/keps/349-all-or-nothing), because it waits and stops the whole scheduler.
Also, [#14089](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/kueue/pull/14089) removed `SchedulerLongRequeueInterval`, so it doesn't apply here.

### Comment by [@kaushik229](https://github.com/kaushik229) — 2026-10-19T22:33:44Z

Thanks @sohankunkerkar, that's a fair ask! I think the specific numbers from one setup matter less than the ratio that drives this: how fast Kueue releases Pods versus how fast they can actually be bound. Whenever the scheduler is slow, binding only a single-digit number of Pods per second, a single Job can still release its entire parallelism at once. That can be thousands of Pods, and on its own it becomes the whole backlog.

On `AdmissionGatedBy`: a gate plus a small controller would work much like the AdmissionCheck-based workaround described in the issue, and for many small Workloads that's probably enough. But it means running and owning another controller in production just for this: deploying, upgrading and keeping it highly available, granting it cluster-wide Pod watch RBAC, and keeping its accounting in sync with Kueue's across versions. Every operator with this problem ends up rebuilding the same thing. It also leaves three gaps, which are the real motivation:

1. **A single large Workload.** The gate is per Workload, so a Job with parallelism in the thousands (e.g., 10,000 Pods) is released all at once. No Workload-level cap can pace that, in-tree or out-of-tree. For Workloads that don't need gang semantics, pacing needs to happen at Pod level after admission, releasing Pods as the backlog drains, similar to how TAS releases Pods through per-Pod scheduling gates.
2. **Capacity that arrives slowly.** With an autoscaler and constrained capacity (e.g., spot instances in a region or account), the effective bind rate is the node arrival rate, which can be minutes rather than seconds. Thousands of unschedulable Pods keep the scheduler and autoscaler busy the whole time. Timeout-based eviction (`unscheduledTimeout`) makes this worse, not better: it evicts and requeues the whole Job, which then floods again when it's re-admitted.
3. **Isolation between tenants.** In a multi-tenant cluster, one ClusterQueue shouldn't be able to slow down the others. Today, whichever ClusterQueue releases the most Pods at once, whether from one large Job or many smaller ones, fills the shared scheduler, and other ClusterQueues wait longer for their Pods to be scheduled even though they're well within their own quota. Quota and FairSharing act at admission, so they don't prevent this, and a single global limit wouldn't either, since one ClusterQueue could still use most of it.

So I'd frame the KEP around Pod-level pacing for non-gang Workloads, decoupled from eviction, with the Workload-level cap as the fallback for gangs. I'd start with a single global limit, since the bottleneck is one scheduler, and extend it to ClusterQueue and Cohort level later, which is where the fairness concern above gets fully addressed. Does that seem like a reasonable scope to you?
